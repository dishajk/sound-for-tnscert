Objects that produce their own light are called \emph{luminous} objects. Objects that do not produce their own light are called \emph{non-luminous} objects. We see non-luminous objects when light reaches them and then enters our eyes.

Some sources of light occur naturally in the world. These are called natural sources of light. The Sun, stars, lightning and fireflies are examples. Some sources of light are made or produced by people. These are called artificial sources of light. Electric bulbs, tube lights, candles and lamps are examples.

\subsection{Think:} Is a fire a natural or an artificial source of light? It can be either. A forest fire can occur naturally, while a fire lit for cooking is started by people

The Moon and planets do not produce their own light. We can see them because they reflect light from the Sun into our eyes. The Moon looks bright because sunlight falls on its surface and is reflected towards us. Planets such as Venus, Mars and Jupiter can also be seen in the night sky because they reflect sunlight.

\subsection{Think:} If the Sun were not shining, would we be able to see the Moon? Why?

\section{Activity: When can you see a non-luminous object?}
\subsection{Materials required:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item cardboard box
    \item non-luminous object (e.g. pen, pencil, sharpener)
    \item torch
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Procedure:}
Put a small non-luminous object inside the cardboard box. Make two small holes. One hole (call this Hole $A$) through which light from a torch can enter and illuminate the object. Second hole (let this be Hole $B$) through which we can look. 

If you cover Hole $A$, can you see the object even though Hole $B$ remains open?

\subsection{Discuss:} Our eye is still looking through Hole $B$. Why can't we see the object?
\subsection{Observe:} To see an object, light must enter our eyes from the object. For a non-luminous object, light from a source first falls on the object. Some of this light is reflected towards our eyes.

\section{Activity: Can light travel through a bent path?}
\subsection{Materials required:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A candle
    \item a dark room
    \item one straight pipe or tube, and a few bent pipes or tubes.
candle
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Procedure:} Light the candle and place it on a table. Look at the flame through the straight pipe. Then try to see the flame through each of the bent pipes. You may move the pipes and change their positions while observing the flame. Compare what you see through the straight and bent pipes.
\subsection{Observe:} You can see the flame through the straight pipe when the candle, pipe and your eye are properly aligned. You cannot see the flame through a bent pipe, even when you try to change its position.

This observation shows that light travels in a straight line when it travels through the same \emph{medium}. A medium is the material through which light travels, such as air, water or glass.

\section{Activity: Three Torches and One Hole}
\subsection{Materials required:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Three torches
\item a cardboard sheet with a small hole in the middle
\item a white screen or wall
\item a few books or stands to support the torches and cardboard.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Procedure:} Place the three torches in a row, one beside the other. Place the cardboard sheet with the small hole in front of the torches and a white screen behind the hole. Darken the room as much as possible. Switch on the three torches and adjust their positions so that light from each torch can pass through the hole and reach the screen. Observe the bright spots formed on the screen. Now cover one torch at a time and watch what happens to the bright spots.
\subsection{Observe:} When all three torches are switched on, three bright spots can be seen on the screen. When one torch is covered, the bright spot corresponding to that torch disappears while the other spots remain.
\subsection{Think:} How does the light from each torch reach its particular spot on the screen? What path must the light take to pass through the small hole?

The light from each torch travels through the hole along a straight path and reaches a particular spot on the screen. This gives us evidence that light travels in a straight line when it travels through the same medium.

\section{Activity: Make a Pinhole Camera}
\subsection{Materials required:}
\begin{enumerate} 
  \item Black chart paper 
  \item Tracing paper 
  \item scissors
  \item sticky tape
  \item rubber band
  \item a small piece of aluminium foil used in the kitchen
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Procedure:} Cut a rectangular piece from the black chart paper and roll it into a hollow tube. Secure it with sticky tape or rubber bands so that the tube is firm. The tube can be about 3 cm in diameter and 25 cm long. You can also try different diameters and lengths.

Cover one end of the tube with tracing paper or another translucent material. This will act as the screen on which the image will be formed. Let us call this tube the image tube.

Fold another rectangular piece of black chart paper into a tube that is shorter in length and slightly larger in diameter than the image tube. The image tube should be able to slide smoothly inside it. Cover one end of this tube with a small piece of aluminium foil. Make a very small hole at the centre using a pin. Let us call this tube the pinhole tube.

Insert the image tube into the pinhole tube until the screen just touches the pinhole. Point the pinhole towards a brightly lit object. Look at the translucent screen and slowly move the image tube in or out of the pinhole tube until a clear image appears on the screen. Try viewing different objects and change the distance between the pinhole and the screen.

\subsection{Observe:} An image of the object appears on the translucent screen. The image is upside down. Try changing the distance between the pinhole and the screen. What happens to the size and clarity of the image?

\subsection{Think:} Light from different parts of the object passes through the tiny pinhole and reaches different parts of the screen. How does the straight-line path of light explain why the image is upside down?

Light from each point of the object travels through the tiny hole along a straight path. Light from the top of the object reaches the lower part of the screen, while light from the bottom reaches the upper part. This is why the image formed by a pinhole camera is upside down. The pinhole camera gives us another way to observe that light travels in straight lines.

\section{Activity: How Is a Shadow Formed?}

\subsection{Materials required:} 
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A torch, 
  \item few books, 
  \item a sheet of paper
\end{enumerate}
\subsection{Procedure:}
Paste the sheet of paper on a wall. Let this be our screen. Place the torch on a stack of books so that it shines towards the screen. Place a book or any object between the torch and the screen. Adjust the positions until a clear dark patch appears on the screen. Now remove the object and observe the screen. Next, place the object back and switch off the torch. Compare what you observe in each case.

\subsection{Observe:} When the torch is switched on, the object is between the torch and the screen, a dark patch appears on the screen. When the object is removed, the dark patch disappears. When the torch is switched off, the shadow is no longer visible. 

\subsection{Think:} What is needed to form a shadow? Is the shadow an object that exists on its own?

A shadow is formed when an object blocks some or all of the light reaching a surface. A shadow can be seen on a screen or another surface where the blocked light would otherwise have fallen. A shadow is not an object. It is a region where less light reaches the surface.

\section{Activity: What Changes the Size and Shape of a Shadow?}
\subsection{Procedure:}
In the previous activity, try the following changes one at a time. 1. Move the object closer to the torch. Then move it closer to the screen. 2. Tilt the object without changing its position. 3. Replace the object with another object of the same shape but a different colour. Before each change, predict what will happen to the shadow and then test your prediction.

The size and shape of a shadow depend on the positions of the light source, the object and the screen, and on the shape and orientation of the object. An object placed closer to the light source generally produces a larger shadow. Changing the orientation of the object can change the shape of the shadow. The colour of an opaque object does not determine the size or shape of its shadow.
\section{Modeling light}
We have seen that light travels from a source and can be blocked by an object to form a shadow. But we cannot usually see the path of light as it travels through a room. How can we show the path of light in a drawing?

Since light travels in a straight line through the same medium, we can represent its path using a straight line. We use an arrow on the line to show the direction in which the light travels. This is a simple way of representing the path of light in a particular direction. See Figure~\ref{fig:ray}.
\begin{marginfigure}
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0:2);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{\label{fig:ray}A straight line with an arrow used to represent the path of light is called a ray of light.}
\end{marginfigure}

A light source usually sends light in many directions. We can represent several rays travelling together by drawing a group of rays. Such a group of rays is called a beam of light. A torch produces a beam of light. See Figure~\ref{fig:beam}. Sunlight entering a room through a small opening can also be thought of as a beam of light.

\section{Activity: Law of Reflection}

\subsection{Materials required:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A plane mirror,
\item a sheet of paper,
\item a ruler,
\item a pencil,
\item a protractor,
\item sticky tape,
\item a torch that produces a narrow beam of light.
\end{enumerate}
Place the sheet of paper on a table and draw a straight line across it. Place the mirror along this line and hold it upright using sticky tape or a suitable stand. Choose a point on the mirror line and mark it as O. This is the point where the light will meet the mirror. Draw a line through O at an angle to the mirror. Shine the narrow beam of light along this line so that it reaches the mirror at O. Mark the path of the incoming light and the path of the reflected light on the paper. Remove the mirror and join the marks to draw the two light rays clearly.

Now we need a way to measure the angles made by the two rays. At O, draw a line that makes an angle of 90° with the mirror. This line is called the normal. The angle between the incoming ray and the normal is called the angle of incidence. The angle between the reflected ray and the normal is called the angle of reflection. Use the protractor to measure both angles. Repeat the experiment with the light ray entering the mirror at several different angles.

Observe: The angle of reflection is equal, or very nearly equal, to the angle of incidence. This happens for different angles at which the light ray strikes the mirror.

What do we learn? When a ray of light is reflected from a smooth surface, the angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection. This is called the law of reflection.

Remember that both angles are measured from the normal, not from the mirror surface.

A periscope is particularly useful because it makes students use the ray model.

Have them:

1. Build the periscope.
2. Draw the path of light.
3. Identify the two reflections.

Then ask:

> Why are the mirrors placed at an angle?

That question forces them to connect construction to physics.

# 20. Light pollution

I would **not put this at the end as an isolated topic**.

You already have students recalling it from the previous class, so use it as a **contextual box/activity**.

For example:

> Look at the sky from your home at night. How many stars can you see?

Then compare with:

> Why are fewer stars visible from a brightly lit city?

This can connect sources of light with responsible use of light.

No need to introduce much astronomy here.

# 21. Solar cooker

This is interesting, but I would place it under a small section such as:

### What else can light do?

The important conceptual point is:

> Light can transfer energy to an object and make it warmer.

But be careful: **the chapter's primary conceptual thread is propagation and reflection**, whereas solar cooking introduces energy transfer.

So I would keep it as a short application rather than a major topic.

# 22. Simple numerical problems

I'd make these **only after the angle concept is secure**.

For example:

> A ray strikes a plane mirror at an angle of 30° to the normal. What is the angle of reflection?

Then progressively:

> The angle between the incident ray and reflected ray is 80°. What is the angle of incidence?

That second type is much better pedagogically because students must understand the geometry rather than just substitute into:

**i = r**

I would avoid numerical problems involving complicated mirror geometry at this stage.

# 23. One major gap: "How do we see?"

I think this deserves an explicit section.

You have the ingredients, but the chapter should **close the loop on a misconception introduced at the beginning**.

Early in the chapter ask:

> How do we see the Moon?

Students may say:

> Because the Moon gives light.

Then later:

> How do we see a book?

Eventually establish:

**light source → object → eyes**

For a luminous object:

**source → eyes**

For a non-luminous object:

**source → object → eyes**

This is perhaps the most important conceptual idea tying the chapter together.

---

# 24. Another gap: reflection from ordinary surfaces

I'd explicitly distinguish:

### Regular reflection

Smooth surface → reflected rays remain orderly → clear image.

### Diffuse reflection

Rough surface → reflected rays go in different directions → no clear image.

You don't necessarily need to introduce the terms "regular" and "diffuse" if they aren't in the curriculum, but **the distinction is important** because otherwise students can develop the misconception:

> "Only mirrors reflect light."

A wall reflects light too. We see the wall because light is reflected from it.

This is a very important conceptual bridge between **seeing objects** and **reflection**.

## 26. One further change I strongly recommend

Don't make the chapter a sequence of:

> **Definition → activity → definition → activity → definition**

Instead, repeatedly use:

> **Observe → predict → try → discuss → represent → name the idea → apply it**

For example:

**Don't write:**

> Light travels in a straight line. This is called rectilinear propagation.

Then give an activity.

Instead:

> **Can light turn a corner?**
> Try to send light through these two tubes.
> What do you notice?
> Why do you think this happens?

Then:

> Light travels in a straight line. This is called **rectilinear propagation of light**.

The terminology becomes a **name for something they have already encountered**, rather than another fact to memorise.

---

### The resulting conceptual progression is particularly nice

You can actually make the whole chapter feel like one investigation:

> **We see things because light reaches our eyes.**
> ↓
> **How does light reach them?**
> ↓
> **It travels in straight lines.**
> ↓
> **What if something blocks it?**
> ↓
> **A shadow forms.**
> ↓
> **What if light meets a surface?**
> ↓
> **It can be reflected.**
> ↓
> **Can we predict where it will go?**
> ↓
> **Yes, using rays, normals and angles.**
> ↓
> **Can we use this predictable behaviour?**
> ↓
> **Mirrors, kaleidoscopes and periscopes.**

That gives the chapter a **physics story**, rather than making it a list of prescribed topics.

And I would particularly retain your **secret-message, mirror-labyrinth, shadow-puppetry, kaleidoscope and periscope activities**. They are unusually well suited to Grade 7 because they make students *do something with the concept* rather than merely observe it.
